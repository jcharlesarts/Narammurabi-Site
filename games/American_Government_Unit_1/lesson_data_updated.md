2. LESSON 1 — WHY GOVERNMENT?

Essential Question: What is government, and what are its basic functions?

Central Idea:
Government exists because groups of people need ways to create order, provide leadership, settle conflicts, provide services, protect society, and address problems individuals cannot easily solve alone.

Core Vocabulary:
Government — A system through which leaders make and enforce decisions and laws for a society.
Anarchy — A condition in which there is no functioning government or governing authority.

Functions of Government:

Providing Leadership
* establishing priorities
* defining crimes and punishments
* determining taxes
* directing public policy

Maintaining Order
* police
* courts
* enforcing laws
* settling disputes
* protecting property
* maintaining public peace

Providing Public Services
* public schools
* roads
* sewer systems
* fire departments
* public-health protections
* safety inspections

Providing National Security
* military defense
* protection from foreign attack
* terrorism / national-security institutions

Providing Economic Security & Assistance
* enforcing contracts
* trade policy
* economic regulation
* food assistance
* housing assistance

Instructional through-line:
Students should connect the opening disorder/scarcity scenarios to the problems governments attempt to solve.

3. LESSON 2 — LAW & CODIFICATION

Essential Question: Why do societies write laws down?

Core Vocabulary:
Law — A rule established and enforced by a governing authority.
Code — An organized collection of laws or rules.
Codify — To organize laws or rules and put them into written form.
Codification — The process of organizing and writing laws into a code.
Rule of Law — The principle that laws are consistently enforced and that people and government can be held accountable under the law.
Hammurabi’s Code — An early written legal code associated with Babylonian king Hammurabi.

Central distinction:
Written law ≠ fair law.

Codification can create:
* predictability
* public knowledge
* consistency
* known consequences
* a permanent record

But a written law can still be:
* unfair
* unequal
* discriminatory
* excessively harsh
* unjust

Carry-forward question:
Does the government merely say it has a rule, or does the rule actually constrain power?

4. LESSON 3 — WHO HOLDS POWER?

Essential Question: Who should hold the power to make decisions for society?

Five repeated questions:
1. Who holds power?
2. How is power gained?
3. How much voice do ordinary citizens have?
4. What is one possible advantage?
5. What is one possible disadvantage or risk?

Government Types:

Autocracy
Political power is concentrated in one ruler.

Monarchy
A monarch is head of state; the position is usually inherited.
Absolute monarchy — monarch exercises substantial governing authority.
Constitutional monarchy — monarch is limited and elected institutions perform much or most governing.

Dictatorship
One ruler exercises concentrated political authority, often maintained through force, repression, political control, manipulation of institutions, or elimination of meaningful opposition.

Authoritarian Government
Political power is highly concentrated and meaningful public control is limited.
A government can have a constitution, elections, and political parties and still operate authoritatively if opposition cannot realistically win, leaders cannot be removed, criticism/media are suppressed, or rights exist only on paper.

Oligarchy
A small group holds power, potentially through wealth, family status, military authority, party leadership, social position, or control of institutions.

Democracy
Political authority ultimately derives from the people.

Direct Democracy
Citizens themselves vote on laws or major public decisions.

Representative Democracy
Citizens elect representatives who make public decisions on their behalf.

Republic
For our purposes, public authority is exercised through representatives rather than a hereditary ruler.
Do not teach republic and democracy as opposites. The United States can be described as both a republic and representative democracy.

Instructional rule:
Students should classify from evidence and explain WHY.

5. LESSON 4 — GOVERNMENT IN ACTION: CASE STUDIES

Essential Question: How does the way political power is organized affect ordinary people?

Repeated case questions:
1. Who has the power?
2. How did that person/group get power?
3. How much voice do citizens have?
4. What can citizens do if they disagree?
5. What is one possible strength?
6. What is one possible risk?
7. What government type does this best represent?
8. What evidence proves it?

Access supports:
* short reading chunks
* same questions every time
* emphasized clues
* teacher-modeled first case
* partner/small-group analysis
* ONE → FEW → MANY visual
* sentence frames

Fictional cases currently include:
* Kingdom of Ardin — monarchy
* Bellweather — direct democracy
* Norvia — representative democracy / republic
* Meridia — oligarchy
* Vargos — dictatorship / authoritarian
* Selden — formally democratic institutions but authoritarian operation

Real-world cases may be used alongside fictional cases, but students should classify evidence rather than flags or country names.

Core debrief:
POWER CONCENTRATED ←————————→ POWER SHARED

Do not reduce analysis to Democracy = good / Dictatorship = bad.
Examine participation, efficiency, rights, accountability, limits, stability, representation, and risks of concentrated power.

6. LESSON 5 — ROAD TO SELF-RULE

Essential Question: How do people develop an expectation that they deserve a voice in government?

Central Idea:
American ideas of self-government did not suddenly appear in 1776. They developed through English political traditions, agreements, representative institutions, colonial legislatures, and long periods of substantial local autonomy.

Core Concepts:

Self-Rule
The ability of a people or community to govern themselves and participate in decisions affecting them.

Magna Carta
Government power can be limited; even a ruler is not necessarily above established law.
Shorthand: Magna Carta → limits the ruler.

Mayflower Compact
Members of a community agree to establish a government and obey shared rules.
Shorthand: Mayflower Compact → agreement for self-government.

House of Burgesses / Colonial Legislatures
Colonists gained experience choosing representatives and participating in local lawmaking.
Shorthand: House of Burgesses → practice with representative government.

Salutary Neglect
Relatively loose British enforcement allowed colonists to become accustomed to managing many of their own affairs.
Shorthand: Salutary Neglect → colonists become accustomed to autonomy.

Representation
Having people chosen to speak, vote, or make political decisions on behalf of others.

Original cause/effect chain:
Limits on rulers
↓
Agreements for self-government
↓
Practice electing representatives
↓
Habit of managing local affairs
↓
Growing expectation of self-rule

9. MODULE 6 — ENLIGHTENMENT: WHERE SHOULD POWER COME FROM?

Big Question:
Where should government power come from, and what does government owe the people it governs?

Core Vocabulary:
* Enlightenment
* natural law
* natural rights
* social contract
* consent of the governed
* popular sovereignty
* right to revolution
* limited government

Core Thinkers:

John Locke
Highest priority for Declaration connection.
Students should recognize:
* natural rights
* government exists to protect rights
* legitimate political power depends on consent
* people may alter or replace a government that violates the social contract

Shorthand:
Locke → rights + consent + right to replace abusive government

Thomas Hobbes
Useful contrast with Lesson 1:
* disorder/insecurity creates a need for government
* people surrender some freedom for order/security
* Hobbes supports stronger governing authority than Locke

Shorthand:
Hobbes → security requires strong authority

Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Useful for:
* popular sovereignty
* political authority rooted in the people
* social contract

Shorthand:
Rousseau → sovereignty belongs to the people

Montesquieu
Useful for later Constitution material:
* separation of powers
* dividing governmental authority as protection against concentrated power

Shorthand:
Montesquieu → divide power

Instructional priority:
Do not make this a philosopher biography unit.

Recurring task:
IDEA → EVIDENCE → AMERICAN GOVERNMENT CONNECTION

11. DECLARATION OF INDEPENDENCE — REQUIRED ANALYSIS

Assessed Standard:
9-12.GV.2.CC.B — Analyze the Declaration of Independence to determine the influence of classical and Enlightenment thought on revolutionary ideals.

Students need to connect passages to principles.

Natural / Unalienable Rights
People possess rights that government does not simply grant.
Declaration clue: Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness.

Consent of the Governed
Legitimate government receives authority from the people.
Declaration clue: governments derive just powers from the consent of the governed.

Social Contract
Government exists for purposes connected to the people it governs, especially protection of rights.

Right to Revolution / Alter or Abolish
If government becomes destructive of those purposes, the people may alter or replace it.

Popular Sovereignty
Political authority ultimately originates with the people.

Grievances
The Declaration states principles and then argues that British government violated those principles. Grievances function as evidence supporting independence.

Required Student Skill:
Given a passage from the Declaration, students should be able to:
1. identify the principle
2. explain it in student-friendly language
3. connect it to Enlightenment thought, especially Locke
4. explain how it supports the argument for independence

High-value passage ideas:
* all men are created equal
* unalienable rights
* Life, Liberty and pursuit of Happiness
* governments instituted to secure rights
* consent of the governed
* right of the people to alter or abolish government

Study architecture should support Locke / Enlightenment → Declaration primary-source comparisons.

16. QUIZ / STUDY QUESTION BANK

Existing high-value targets:

Functions:

Correct concept: Providing Public Services.

Maintaining Order:
Neighbors take a property dispute to court.
Correct concept: Maintaining Order.

Codification:
A society gathers, organizes, and records laws in a written legal code.
Correct concept: Codification.

Rule of Law:
Consistent enforcement and accountability under law.
Correct concept: Rule of Law.

Monarchy:
Traditional clue — head-of-state position commonly inherited rather than won through public election.

Oligarchy:
Small group of wealthy/military leaders controls major decisions.

Direct Democracy:
Citizens themselves vote on proposed laws.

Representative Democracy:
Citizens elect lawmakers who debate and vote on laws.

Authoritarian Government:
Formal constitution/elections/parties may exist while opposition is jailed, media closed, and ruling group cannot realistically lose power.

Magna Carta:
Ruler’s power can be limited.

Mayflower Compact:
Community can agree to govern itself under shared rules.

House of Burgesses:
Experience with representative government.

Salutary Neglect:
Colonists became accustomed to managing many of their own affairs.

Short Response:
Choose two types of government. Explain one important difference in who holds power and how citizens participate.

OR:

Choose two case studies. Explain one important difference in how political power is distributed and how that difference affects citizens.

Success Criteria:
1. correctly identifies both systems
2. identifies who holds power
3. explains difference in citizen participation
4. provides evidence/example

25. INSTRUCTIONAL GUARDRAILS

Don’t use rote definition questions when a short scenario can test the same concept better.

Ask “Who holds power?” constantly.

Require students to identify the clue/evidence, not merely the label.

Keep reading chunks accessible to ELL students without reducing the intellectual question.

Reuse directions and question structures. The thinking should be difficult; figuring out what the worksheet wants shouldn’t be.

Use fictional countries to isolate concepts and real countries to transfer them.

Don’t pretend actual governments fit perfectly into textbook boxes.

Don’t reduce analysis to Democracy = good / Dictatorship = bad.

Instead examine:
* participation
* efficiency
* rights
* accountability
* limits
* stability
* representation
* risks of concentrated power

Don’t teach that elections automatically mean democracy.

Don’t teach that a written constitution automatically means limited government.

Don’t teach that written law automatically means fair law.

Use autocracy as an umbrella/stair-step concept.

Treat republic and representative democracy as overlapping concepts rather than opposites.

Keep English academic vocabulary visible while translations provide access.

Don’t put future district vocabulary into graded review merely because it is on the district list.

Keep CURRENTLY TAUGHT and PRE-MIDTERM PLANNED as the active content statuses.

For the first assessment:
Small grade, serious thinking.

Overall progression:

WHY GOVERNMENT?
→ WHY LAW?
→ WHO RULES?
→ WHAT DOES THAT DO TO PEOPLE?
→ WHY WOULD PEOPLE EXPECT SELF-RULE?
→ WHERE SHOULD LEGITIMATE POWER COME FROM?
→ WHEN MAY PEOPLE REJECT A GOVERNMENT?
→ HOW DOES THE DECLARATION TURN THOSE IDEAS INTO AN ARGUMENT?

That progression should remain the backbone of the study site.
