2. LESSON 1 — Why Government?

Essential Question

What is government, and what are its basic functions?

Central Idea

Government exists because groups of people need ways to:

* create order
* provide leadership
* settle conflicts
* provide services
* protect society
* address problems that individuals cannot easily solve alone

Opening Scenarios

A. School Without Authority

You arrive at school and settle into your desk, but there is no teacher. Eventually, you realize there are no teachers, administrators, or security — only students.

Nobody can communicate with anyone outside the building, and the doors are locked.

Some of the rowdiest students begin planning a “day of pandemonium.”

You do not know what they intend.

B. Disaster and Looting

After a major hurricane, the power, phone, and internet lines in your city have been out for more than a week.

You hear rumors that armed groups are roaming the streets.

You have spent four sleepless nights helping guard your parents’ store from looters.

C. Famine and Scarce Supplies

Your country is experiencing a devastating famine.

A truck carrying flour, sugar, water, and medicine is scheduled to arrive in the center of town.

Hundreds of additional people arrive from another town. People begin cutting in line and fighting.

The truck drivers become afraid of the crowd and consider leaving.

Student Questions

For the assigned scenario:

1. What are two words that describe this situation?
2. How might you feel?
3. What kinds of people or groups might “do well” in this environment?
4. Who could control or prevent this situation?
5. How could they control or prevent it?

The scenarios establish the conceptual problem before students receive the vocabulary.

⸻

Core Vocabulary

Government

A system through which leaders make and enforce decisions and laws for a society.

Anarchy

A condition in which there is no functioning government or governing authority.

⸻

Functions of Government

Providing Leadership

Government leaders establish priorities and make decisions on behalf of society.

Examples:

* deciding what actions are crimes
* establishing punishments
* determining taxes
* directing public policy

Maintaining Order

Government establishes and enforces rules and provides ways to resolve conflicts.

Examples:

* police
* courts
* enforcing laws
* settling disputes
* protecting property
* maintaining public peace

Providing Public Services

Government provides services that individuals may be unable or unwilling to provide effectively on their own.

Examples:

* public schools
* roads
* sewer systems
* fire departments
* public-health protections
* safety inspections

Providing National Security

Government protects the country and its population from outside threats.

Examples:

* military defense
* foreign attack
* terrorism
* national security institutions

Providing Economic Security & Assistance

Government establishes economic rules, promotes stability, enforces agreements, and may assist people in need.

Examples:

* enforcing contracts
* trade policy
* economic regulation
* food assistance
* housing assistance

⸻

Apply-It Organizer

For each function:

Purpose of Government	Definition in Your Own Words	Example from Your Family	Example from This Classroom/School	Example from City, State, or Country

This becomes both application and later retrieval material.

Study Questions

* A city operates fire departments and maintains roads. Which government function is most visible?
* Courts settle a dispute between two neighbors. Which function?
* The military protects the country from an attack. Which function?
* Government provides food assistance to families. Which function?
* Which government function was most obviously missing from one of our opening scenarios?
* How might effective government have changed that scenario?

3. LESSON 2 — Law & Codification

Essential Question

Why do societies write laws down?

Hook / Bridge

Think of a rule everyone is supposed to follow — but the rule isn’t written down anywhere.

Questions:

* What problems could this create?
* Could different people remember the rule differently?
* Could someone in authority suddenly change it?
* Would everyone know the consequence for breaking it?
* Who gets to decide what the rule “really” means?

This bridges Lesson 1’s need for order into the development of law.

⸻

Core Vocabulary

Law

A rule established and enforced by a governing authority.

Code

An organized collection of laws or rules.

Codify

To organize laws or rules and put them into written form.

Codification

The process of organizing and writing laws into a code.

Rule of Law

The principle that laws are consistently enforced and that people and government can be held accountable under the law.

Hammurabi’s Code

An early written legal code associated with Babylonian king Hammurabi.

The important instructional point isn’t “Hammurabi invented law.”

It’s:

People were codifying rules and consequences thousands of years ago.

⸻

The Central Distinction

Written law ≠ fair law.

Codification can create:

* predictability
* public knowledge
* consistency
* known consequences
* a permanent record

But a written law can still be:

* unfair
* unequal
* discriminatory
* excessively harsh
* unjust

That’s a distinction worth carrying all the way through the Constitution unit:

Does the government say it has a rule, or does the rule actually constrain power?

⸻

Hammurabi Gallery Walk

For each law/situation:

1. What do you think should happen?
2. What does Hammurabi’s Code say should happen?
3. Is that fair or unfair? Why?
4. What problem is the law trying to solve?
5. What does the law tell us about Babylonian society?

Cases can include things like:

* a son striking his father
* physical injury / “eye for an eye”
* physician malpractice
* builder liability
* compensation for injury

Study Questions

* A society gathers its rules and records them in an organized written legal code. What is this process called?
* Why might written laws be more predictable than unwritten rules?
* What does rule of law mean?
* Why can codification matter even when an individual law is unfair?
* Does writing a law down make it just? Why or why not?

4. LESSON 3 — Who Holds Power?

Essential Question

Who should hold the power to make decisions for society?

Do Now — Line Debate

Is it better to have a single, strong leader or to have shared power among many different people? Defend your answer.

Physical continuum:

ONE STRONG LEADER ←————————→ POWER SHARED AMONG MANY

Follow-up questions:

* Why did you stand where you did?
* What’s one advantage of concentrating power?
* What’s one danger?
* What’s one advantage of sharing power?
* What’s one danger?
* Did anybody’s argument make you move?

This means the vocabulary emerges from a problem students have already considered.

⸻

Our Five Repeated Questions

For every government system:

1. Who holds power?
2. How is power gained?
3. How much voice do ordinary citizens have?
4. What is one possible advantage?
5. What is one possible disadvantage or risk?

These should become almost automatic.

⸻

Government Types

Autocracy

Core concept: political power is concentrated in one ruler.

We’re using autocracy as a useful umbrella/stair-step concept, rather than forcing it into a completely separate box from monarchy/dictatorship.

Possible advantages:

* quick decisions
* clear leadership

Possible risks:

* little citizen control
* abuse of power
* few meaningful checks on the ruler

⸻

Monarchy

Who holds power?
A monarch is the head of state.

How is the position gained?
Usually inheritance through a royal family.

Important distinction:

Absolute Monarchy

The monarch exercises substantial governing authority.

Constitutional Monarchy

The monarch’s authority is limited and elected institutions perform much or most governing.

Possible advantages:

* continuity
* clear succession
* stability

Possible risks:

* head of state isn’t selected by voters
* an absolute monarch may face few effective restraints

⸻

Dictatorship

Who holds power?
One ruler exercises concentrated political authority.

Power may be seized or maintained through:

* military force
* repression
* political control
* manipulation of institutions
* elimination of meaningful opposition

Possible advantage:

* decisions can be made quickly

Major risks:

* limited political freedom
* few meaningful limits on the ruler
* suppression of opposition

⸻

Authoritarian Government

Core idea: political power is highly concentrated and meaningful public control is limited.

Very important:

A government can have a constitution, elections, and political parties and still function as an authoritarian government.

Things students should learn to look for:

* Can the opposition realistically win?
* Can leaders actually be removed?
* Can citizens criticize government?
* Is independent media allowed?
* Are political opponents jailed?
* Are rights enforced or merely written down?

This directly reinforces Lesson 2:

Something existing on paper does not mean it exists in practice.

⸻

Oligarchy

Who holds power?
A small group.

That group might derive its influence from:

* wealth
* family status
* military authority
* party leadership
* social position
* control of major institutions

Possible advantage:

* a small group may make decisions efficiently
* members may have specialized expertise

Risks:

* ordinary people have little influence
* the ruling group may govern for its own benefit

⸻

Democracy

Core idea: political authority ultimately derives from the people.

But democracy has multiple forms.

⸻

Direct Democracy

Citizens directly participate in making laws or major public decisions.

Key clue:

Citizens themselves vote on the issue.

Possible advantages:

* extremely direct participation
* voters exercise immediate influence

Possible disadvantages:

* difficult at very large scale
* slower decision-making
* majority rule can create minority-rights problems

⸻

Representative Democracy

Citizens elect representatives who make public decisions on their behalf.

Key clue:

Citizens elect someone else to make laws.

Possible advantages:

* practical for large societies
* representatives can specialize
* citizens can replace representatives through elections

Possible risks:

* representatives may not always reflect constituent preferences
* citizens don’t vote directly on every policy

⸻

Republic

For our purposes:

Public authority is exercised through representatives rather than a hereditary ruler.

We should not teach “republic” and “democracy” as opposites.

The United States can reasonably be described as both:

* a republic
* a representative democracy

5. LESSON 4 — Government in Action: Case Studies

Essential Question

How does the way political power is organized affect ordinary people?

This replaces the more complicated building simulation.

Rather than making our ELL students simultaneously decode elaborate procedural directions and understand a new political concept, the procedural structure stays consistent while the content changes.

⸻

Do Now

Which type of government gives citizens the most direct voice? Which gives citizens the least? Explain.

⸻

Repeated Case-Study Questions

Every case asks:

1. Who has the power?
2. How did that person/group get power?
3. How much voice do citizens have?
4. What can citizens do if they disagree?
5. What’s one possible strength?
6. What’s one possible risk?
7. What government type does this best represent?
8. What evidence proves it?

⸻

ELL / Access Supports

* short reading chunks
* same questions every time
* important clues visually emphasized
* first case modeled by teacher
* partner/small-group analysis before independent response
* ONE → FEW → MANY visual
* sentence frames

Sentence Frames

This is a ______ because ______ holds power.

The strongest evidence is ______.

Citizens have more/less voice because ______.

One possible advantage is ______.

One possible risk is ______.

⸻

Fictional Case Bank

Kingdom of Ardin

The country’s head of state inherits the position from a parent. The monarch appoints important officials and can make many major decisions without a national election.

Classification: Monarchy
Power: Monarch
Evidence: Leadership is inherited and substantial governing authority belongs to the monarch.

⸻

Bellweather

Adult citizens vote directly on major proposed laws several times each year. An elected administrator carries out the laws but cannot create them alone.

Classification: Direct Democracy
Power: Citizens directly
Evidence: Citizens themselves vote on laws.

⸻

Norvia

Citizens elect lawmakers every four years. Those lawmakers debate and pass national laws. Voters can replace their representatives during later elections.

Classification: Representative Democracy / Republic
Power: Elected representatives deriving authority from voters
Evidence: Citizens choose representatives instead of voting directly on each law.

⸻

Meridia

A small council of wealthy business and military leaders controls the country’s major decisions. Ordinary citizens do not choose the council.

Classification: Oligarchy
Power: Small elite group
Evidence: A small group controls political decision-making.

⸻

Vargos

One leader controls the military, courts, and national media. Political opponents can be arrested, and voters cannot realistically remove the leader.

Classification: Dictatorship / Authoritarian Government
Power: One ruler
Evidence: Power is concentrated in one person with few meaningful limits.

⸻

Selden

Selden has a written constitution, elections, and several political parties.

However, the government closes opposition news organizations, arrests major critics, and uses government power to prevent the ruling party from realistically losing an election.

Classification: Authoritarian Government

Evidence: Democratic institutions exist formally, but meaningful political competition and dissent are suppressed.

⸻

Real-World Cases

Our classroom material can also use recognizable real examples — including the United States and North Korea — alongside fictional cases.

The website should deliberately mix fictional and real cases.

Why?

Because I don’t want this:

🇰🇵 → dictatorship

I want:

“The leader cannot realistically be removed through elections, opposition is suppressed, and power is concentrated…”

→ Therefore, authoritarian/dictatorial government.

Students should classify evidence, not flags.

⸻

Power Continuum Debrief

POWER CONCENTRATED ←————————→ POWER SHARED

Questions:

* Which system might make decisions fastest?
* Which gives citizens the most direct participation?
* Which creates the greatest risk of unchecked power?
* Does efficiency equal fairness?
* Does voting automatically make a government democratic?
* Can a government appear democratic on paper but actually operate differently?
* What tradeoffs appear as power becomes more concentrated or more dispersed?

Collected Response

Choose two case studies. Explain one important difference in who holds power and how that difference affects citizens. Use evidence from both cases.

6. LESSON 5 — Road to Self-Rule

Essential Question

How do people develop an expectation that they deserve a voice in government?

Do Now

If you have been allowed to make your own decisions for years, how would you react if somebody suddenly took that power away? Explain.

Central Idea

American ideas of self-government did not suddenly appear in 1776.

They developed over time through:

* English political traditions
* agreements
* representative institutions
* colonial legislatures
* long periods of substantial local autonomy

⸻

Major Concepts

Self-Rule

The ability of a people or community to govern themselves and participate in decisions affecting them.

⸻

Magna Carta

Big connection:

Government power can be limited.

Even the ruler is not necessarily above established law.

Study shorthand

Magna Carta → limits the ruler

⸻

Mayflower Compact

Big connection:

Members of a community agree to establish a government and obey rules created for their common good.

Study shorthand

Mayflower Compact → agreement for self-government

⸻

House of Burgesses / Colonial Legislatures

Big connection:

Colonists gained experience choosing representatives and participating in local lawmaking.

Study shorthand

House of Burgesses → practice with representative government

⸻

Salutary Neglect

Big connection:

Long periods of relatively loose British enforcement allowed colonists to become accustomed to managing many of their own affairs.

Study shorthand

Salutary Neglect → colonists become accustomed to autonomy

⸻

Representation

Having people chosen to speak, vote, or make political decisions on behalf of others.

⸻

Cause-and-Effect Chain

Limits on rulers

↓

Agreements for self-government

↓

Practice electing representatives

↓

Habit of managing local affairs

↓

Growing expectation of self-rule

This tees up our later conflict beautifully:

What happens when a population becomes accustomed to political autonomy…and then believes somebody is taking it away?

18. INSTRUCTIONAL GUARDRAILS

These are probably the most important part of the whole document.

Don’t use rote definition questions when a short scenario can test the same concept better.

Ask “Who holds power?” constantly.

Require students to identify the clue/evidence, not merely the label.

Keep reading chunks accessible to ELL students without reducing the intellectual question.

Reuse directions and question structures. The thinking should be difficult; figuring out what the worksheet wants shouldn’t be.

Use fictional countries to isolate concepts and real countries to transfer them.

Don’t pretend actual governments fit perfectly into textbook boxes.

Don’t reduce the analysis to:

Democracy = good
Dictatorship = bad

Instead examine:

* participation
* efficiency
* rights
* accountability
* limits
* stability
* representation
* risks of concentrated power

Don’t teach that elections automatically mean democracy.

Don’t teach that a written constitution automatically means limited government.

Don’t teach that written law automatically means fair law.

Use autocracy as an umbrella/stair-step concept.

Treat republic and representative democracy as overlapping concepts rather than opposites.

Keep English academic vocabulary visible while translations provide access.

Don’t put future district vocabulary into graded review merely because it’s on the district list.

And for this first assessment:

Small grade, serious thinking.

That, I think, is the backbone of what we’re building. It gives us a nice progression from WHY GOVERNMENT? → WHY LAW? → WHO RULES? → WHAT DOES THAT DO TO PEOPLE? → WHY WOULD PEOPLE EXPECT SELF-RULE?, which then hands us naturally into Enlightenment and independence rather than making early American government feel like a procession of vocabulary terms.
